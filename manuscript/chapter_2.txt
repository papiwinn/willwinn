Chapter 2
Oshkosh by Gosh
Late March–June 1943

The train that carried Austin north out of Florida in the final days of March 1943 climbed out of the subtropical heat and into a different country. By the time the cars reached Wisconsin the air had turned sharp and the landscape had changed from palm trees and white sand to bare fields still waiting for spring. He stepped off in Oshkosh as a member of the 96th College Training Detachment, one of dozens of similar programs the Army Air Forces had scattered across the country. The idea was simple and ambitious: take young men who had survived basic training, put them on a college campus for academic ground school, and at the same time begin teaching them to fly.
Oshkosh State Teachers College became his new world. The routine was still military—formations, inspections, physical training—but the days now included classrooms, textbooks, and the first real promise of an airplane.

What a College Training Detachment Was
In 1943 the Army Air Forces faced an enormous need for pilots. Rather than keep every cadet on crowded military bases, the service contracted with colleges across the United States. Cadets lived under military discipline, studied mathematics, physics, navigation, and weather, and received their first dual instruction in light training planes, often Piper Cubs or similar aircraft, under civilian instructors supervised by the Army. For many young men it was the first time the abstract idea of “becoming a pilot” turned into the concrete sensation of leaving the ground.

Austin’s early letters from Oshkosh show a young man adjusting to the new rhythm and still carrying the weight of recent loss. The Wisconsin spring was slow to arrive. Mornings could still be cold enough that the cadets stamped their feet on the parade ground. Classes filled the middle of the day. In the afternoons or early evenings came the thing he had been waiting for since Miami Beach: the flight line.
The first dual flights left him almost speechless with excitement. He wrote home about the sensation of the little trainer lifting off, about the instructor’s calm voice in the headphones, about looking down on the Wisconsin countryside from a few hundred feet. When the day finally came for his first solo, the letter that followed carried a quiet, unmistakable pride. He had taken the plane up alone, flown the pattern, and brought it safely back to earth. The farm boy from Henning had become a pilot in the smallest but most important sense of the word.
Daily life still contained the ordinary frictions of military existence. The food was better than basic training but still institutional. There were inspections, demerits, and the constant pressure to keep grades high enough to stay in the program. Weather delayed flying more than once. Boredom crept in on long evenings when the only entertainment was a letter from home or a movie in town. Through it all he continued to write to his mother, his sister, and the rest of the family, asking about the farm and the livestock and the ordinary details of life in Tennessee. The connection to home remained the steadying force beneath the new experiences.

AXIS FORCES IN NORTH AFRICA SURRENDER
Tunis and Bizerte Fall – Entire German-Italian Army Captured
— May 13, 1943
While Austin was logging his early solo hours over the fields of Wisconsin, the long campaign in North Africa came to an end. The newspapers that reached the college training detachment carried banner headlines. More than a quarter of a million Axis soldiers laid down their arms. For the first time since the United States had entered the war, an entire theater had been cleared of the enemy. The men in Oshkosh read the news between classes and flight periods. It felt distant, yet it mattered. The planes they were learning to fly would one day carry the war to the next battleground.
A few days later another story competed for attention.
RAF LANCASTER BOMBERS SMASH GERMAN DAMS
Dambusters Raid Floods Ruhr Valley in Daring Night Attack
— May 17, 1943
The British had struck deep into industrial Germany with a special bouncing-bomb attack. The photographs of the breached dams circulated quickly. For aviation cadets the raid was both inspiring and sobering—an example of what carefully trained crews and specialized aircraft could accomplish.
Through April, May, and into June, Austin’s letters grew more confident. He wrote of improving grades, of longer flights, of the satisfaction of handling the airplane alone. He still sent laundry home, still requested small items, still signed every letter with the same steady affection. But the tone had shifted. The wide-eyed recruit of Miami Beach was becoming a young man who could speak of airspeed and altitude and the feel of the controls with growing authority.
By late June the college training phase was drawing to a close. Orders would soon send him west again, this time to California and the next stage of the long pipeline: classification and pre-flight school at Santa Ana. The months in Oshkosh had done their work. He had studied, he had flown, and he had begun to believe that the silver wings he had dreamed of might actually be within reach.
The last letters from Wisconsin still asked about the crops and the family. Home remained the fixed point. Everything else—the classrooms, the flight line, the distant victories in Africa—was simply the path that was carrying him farther away from it, one solo flight at a time.contine
