Chapter 7
Over the Empire
January–August 1945

In early 1945 Austin crossed the Pacific and arrived in the Mariana Islands. The 19th Bomb Group was based on a newly carved airfield surrounded by jungle. Living conditions were primitive at first—tents, outdoor showers, meals eaten from mess kits under the open sky. The heat and humidity were constant. Rain turned the ground to mud. Yet the runway was operational, and the B-29s were flying.
Combat began quickly. Austin flew as co-pilot and later as Airplane Commander on missions against the Japanese home islands. The letters describe long hours in the air, the tension of the bomb run, searchlights, flak, the occasional fighter attack, and the deep fatigue that followed a fifteen- or eighteen-hour mission. He wrote of emergency landings at Iwo Jima, of engines feathered, of holes in the wings, and of the quiet relief of seeing the home field again.
He recorded the fire raids on Tokyo, Osaka, Nagoya, and Kobe. He noted the promotion to Airplane Commander and the responsibility of leading his own crew. He received the Air Medal and later the Distinguished Flying Cross. Through it all he continued to write home as often as the schedule and censorship allowed—asking about the family, the farm, the new baby, and the ordinary details of life in Tennessee.

Austin was in the air or on the flight line for many of the final missions. On 2 September 1945 he flew one of the great demonstration flights over Tokyo while the formal surrender was being signed in the bay below. The war was over.
The letters from these months are steady, professional, and still deeply connected to home. He never dramatized the danger. He simply recorded what happened, asked after the family, and signed every letter with love.
