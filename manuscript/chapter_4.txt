Chapter 4
The Real Thing
October 1943–January 1944

In early October 1943 the orders finally came. Austin left Santa Ana and traveled north into California’s Central Valley to Eagle Field, near the small town of Dos Palos. Here the abstract promise of flight became concrete. The aircraft waiting on the flight line were Ryan PT-22 Recruit trainers—open-cockpit, fabric-covered airplanes with a single radial engine and the clean, honest lines of a machine built to teach young men how to fly.
The first dual flights with an instructor quickly gave way to the moment every cadet waited for: the instructor climbing out, looking the student in the eye, and saying the words that meant he was on his own. Austin’s letters home from those early weeks at Eagle Field pulse with the pure excitement of a young man who has just been trusted with an airplane.
He wrote of the daily schedule—early mornings on the flight line, ground school in between, the constant accumulation of hours. He described the feel of the stick and rudder, the way the little Ryan responded, the satisfaction of a clean landing. Mail from home was often delayed, and the wait for letters became a small daily frustration. Yet even that frustration was tempered by the knowledge that he was finally doing the thing he had joined the Army Air Forces to do.

Primary Flight Training
Primary was the first true flying phase. Cadets learned basic aircraft control, takeoffs and landings, stalls, spins, and simple cross-country navigation. The Ryan PT-22 was forgiving but demanded attention. Instructors were civilian pilots under military contract. Solo time was the measure of progress. A cadet who could not solo safely was washed out. Those who succeeded moved on with growing confidence and a logbook that began to fill with real hours.

By November the hours were adding up. Austin wrote of thirty or more flying hours, of time in the Link Trainer practicing instrument work on the ground, of check rides that decided whether a man continued or went home. The Central Valley weather could turn suddenly, grounding the flight line for a day or two. Food remained a steady topic. Evenings were short. Lights-out came early because the next day’s flying began before dawn.
Christmas approached while he was still at Eagle Field. Packages arrived from Tennessee. He shared cookies with the other cadets and wrote detailed thanks. The war felt both distant and immediate.

MARINES TAKE TARAWA AFTER BLOODY THREE-DAY BATTLE
Heavy Casualties Reported in First Major Assault on Japanese-Held Atoll
— Late November 1943
While Austin practiced landings in the California valley, the Pacific war delivered one of its grimmest lessons. The Marine assault on Tarawa produced casualty figures that shocked the home front. For aviation cadets the news was a reminder that the airplanes they were learning to fly would soon operate in the same unforgiving theater.
A few weeks later another announcement reshaped the European war.
EISENHOWER NAMED SUPREME ALLIED COMMANDER
American General Chosen to Lead Coming Invasion of Europe
— December 1943
In December Austin graduated from primary and transferred south to War Eagle Field near Lancaster, California, for Basic training. The aircraft changed. The open-cockpit Ryan gave way to the heavier, more powerful Vultee BT-13 Valiant—a low-wing monoplane with a canopy, greater speed, and the instruments needed for more advanced work. Night flying began. Instrument training intensified. Formation work appeared on the schedule.
The letters from Lancaster show a cadet settling into the new demands. He wrote of longer flights, of the different feel of the BT-13, of the cold desert nights and the early morning briefings. Christmas and New Year’s passed with packages from home and the quiet knowledge that another year of war had begun. He was no longer a beginner. The logbook was growing. The next step—Advanced training—already loomed.
Through the entire period the letters home remained steady and affectionate. He still asked about the farm, the livestock, and the family. He still signed every letter with love. The boy who had left Tennessee in January 1943 was becoming a pilot in fact as well as in name, yet the fixed point of home never shifted.
