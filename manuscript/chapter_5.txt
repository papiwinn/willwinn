Chapter 5
Wings and a Commission
February–April 1944

In February 1944 Austin left the California desert and traveled east to Marfa Army Air Field in the high, dry country of West Texas. Advanced training had begun. The single-engine trainers of primary and basic gave way to twin-engine aircraft, and the flying became more complex, more demanding, and more clearly pointed toward combat.
Marfa sat in a wide, windswept valley surrounded by mountains. The air was thin and clear. Here the cadets learned to handle multi-engine airplanes, to fly on instruments for longer periods, to navigate cross-country at night, and to operate as part of a crew rather than as solitary pilots.
The letters from Marfa show a young man fully absorbed in the work. He wrote of long training flights, of the different feel of two engines, of the concentration required to keep a heavier airplane stable and on course. Check rides grew more serious. Yet Austin’s tone remained steady and increasingly confident.

Advanced Twin-Engine Training
At this stage cadets flew twin-engine trainers designed to bridge the gap between basic flying and the heavy bombers they would eventually crew. The emphasis shifted from pure stick-and-rudder skill to systems management, fuel planning, radio procedure, and crew coordination. Night cross-countries tested navigation under difficult conditions. Instrument flying under the hood became routine. Those who completed the course received their silver pilot wings and a commission as second lieutenant.

Daily life at Marfa retained the familiar military structure. The West Texas weather could change quickly. Wind and dust storms occasionally grounded the aircraft. On clear nights the stars were brilliant. Food was adequate. Packages from home were shared. Letters remained the primary link to the world beyond the base.
Austin continued to write about the farm, the family, and the ordinary details of life in Tennessee. The emotional thread that had run through every letter since the Dixieland was unbroken.

ALLIES CONTINUE SLOW ADVANCE IN ITALY
Anzio Beachhead Holds as Cassino Fighting Grinds On
— Winter–Spring 1944
While the cadets at Marfa logged their advanced hours, the war in Italy remained a grinding struggle. Progress was measured in yards rather than miles. For young pilots the news was a reminder that the European war would not end quickly.
On 15 April 1944 the course at Marfa came to an end. Austin graduated from Advanced flying school. In a ceremony that mixed formality with deep personal satisfaction, he received the silver pilot wings of the Army Air Forces and a commission as a second lieutenant. The farm boy who had boarded a train in Tennessee fifteen months earlier was now an officer and a rated pilot.
The letters written around graduation carry a restrained but unmistakable pride. He described the wings, the new rank, and the brief leave that followed. For a short time he was able to go home, to see the family, and to let the reality of what he had accomplished settle in. The visit was short. The war was waiting.
When the leave ended he reported for the next phase: transition to heavy bombers. Austin H. Winn, second lieutenant, pilot, packed his gear once more and moved on.
The letters home still ended with love. Home remained the fixed point. Everything else continued to move.
