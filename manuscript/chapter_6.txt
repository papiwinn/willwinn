Chapter 6
Heavy Iron
Late April–December 1944

After the short leave at home, Austin reported to Roswell Army Air Field in New Mexico for transition to the B-17 Flying Fortress. The four-engine bomber was a different world from the trainers he had flown. It was heavy, complex, and built for the long missions that were already devastating German industry and cities. He learned its systems, practiced instrument flying, and began to understand the demands of operating as part of a ten-man crew.
By summer the next step arrived. He moved through processing at Lincoln, Nebraska, then to Alamogordo, New Mexico, for combat-crew training, and finally to Great Bend, Kansas, where he joined the B-29 Superfortress program with the 19th Bomb Group. The B-29 was the most advanced bomber in the world—pressurized, heavily armed, and designed to carry the air war to Japan from bases still being carved out of the Pacific.
The letters from this long training period show a man settling into professional seriousness. He wrote of long flights, crew formation, the responsibilities of a co-pilot (and later more), and the steady accumulation of experience. Packages and letters from home remained essential. He sent money home, asked about the farm and the family, and shared small details of base life—food, weather, the occasional movie or pass into town.

What the B-29 Meant
The Boeing B-29 Superfortress was a technological leap. It could fly higher and farther than the B-17 or B-24, carry a heavier bomb load, and operate with a pressurized cabin. It was also temperamental and still being refined in 1944. Training in it was intense. Crews learned to manage its four powerful engines, its complex systems, and the long-range navigation required for missions that would eventually stretch across the Pacific.

ALLIES INVADE FRANCE
Massive Air and Sea Assault Opens Second Front in Europe
— June 6, 1944
While Austin trained in the American Southwest and Midwest, the long-awaited invasion of Europe began. D-Day headlines dominated every newspaper. The cadets and young officers followed the maps and understood that the air forces were playing a decisive role both in Europe and, soon, in the Pacific.
By late 1944 the training at Great Bend was nearing its end. Austin’s crew was taking shape. The letters show growing anticipation mixed with the ordinary frustrations of military life—delays, equipment shortages, the endless wait for orders. Christmas 1944 approached with the knowledge that overseas deployment was close.
Home remained the steady reference point. Every letter still carried news of the family and the farm, and every letter still closed with love.
