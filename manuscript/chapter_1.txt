Chapter 1
Leaving Home
January–March 1943

ROOSEVELT, CHURCHILL MEET IN CASABLANCA
Allies Demand Unconditional Surrender of Axis Powers
— Late January 1943
On the last day of January 1943, while President Roosevelt and Prime Minister Churchill were finalizing their demand for the complete surrender of Germany, Italy, and Japan, a twenty-two-year-old farm boy from Lauderdale County, Tennessee, sat on a southbound train and began the first letter of his war.
Austin H. Winn wrote in short, interrupted bursts as the cars of the Dixieland rolled through Georgia.
“It’s now 1 o’clock P.M. Sunday afternoon. I am on the train about Waycross Ga. Had a good nite sleep last nite. The train is stopped nite now but if my handwriting gets to jumping you’ll know we’ve started again. This sure is a nice trip. There are about 10 boys on here going to the same place Miami. They all seem to be nice boys. We’re moving…”
The train carried him away from the familiar red-clay fields and the quiet store on Route 2 outside Henning. Behind him lay the daily rhythm of livestock and cotton and the sudden emptiness of a house that had just sent its son to war. Ahead of him waited a world he could only imagine.
By evening he was in Jacksonville. The Florida heat was already pressing in. “I’m in Jacksonville, Fla., now. The scenery sure is beautiful. Every thing looks fine & dandy. I have a seat with an Ensign of the Navy. The Negroes are washing the windows of the car now. It sure is hot! Will get to Miami tonight about 10:20 I think!”
He arrived in the dark, one more anonymous face among the thousands pouring into South Florida.

What Austin Found in Miami Beach
In early 1942 the Army Air Forces had taken over nearly every hotel and apartment building on Miami Beach. By the time Austin arrived, more than three hundred hotels had been converted into barracks, classrooms, and mess halls. Luxury lobbies became orderly rooms. Swimming pools were used for physical training. The same palm-lined streets that once hosted winter tourists now echoed with marching feet and the commands of drill sergeants. Nearly half a million men would pass through this “Boot Camp in Paradise” before the war ended.

Life settled into a hard, repetitive pattern within days.
“We get up at 5:15 and have to be in formation out in front of our Hotel by 5:30. We march 4 or 5 blocks to eat and back then we go to our rooms on the run and clean everything up spick and span to pass inspection. We go to bed at 9:00 P.M.”
The days were long and physical. On one of the first full training days the men received a battery of shots—tetanus, typhoid, and smallpox—all at once. Austin’s arms were sore for days. They drilled with rifles, learned the Manual of Arms, and stood rigid inspections. The Florida sun was relentless even in February. Salt air clung to the wool uniforms. The food was plentiful but institutional.
Then, while he was still in the middle of basic training, word came from home that his father had died. Austin was granted emergency leave to return to Tennessee for the funeral. The letters written around that time are fewer and more subdued. When he returned to Miami Beach the main body of his original training group had already moved on. He was held over and assigned temporary administrative work—typing records and handling paperwork in an office—until the Army could place him with another unit. The relative freedom of those days felt strange after the rigid schedule of basic training. He swam when he could, walked the beach, and wrote longer letters. One careless afternoon in the sun left him with painful blisters on both shoulders.
By the end of March new orders came. He boarded a train headed north to Oshkosh, Wisconsin, and the next stage of training. The letters from Miami Beach closed the same way they had begun on the Dixieland—with steady affection and the promise to write again soon.
The war had taken him far from the familiar fields of west Tennessee, and it had already cost him his father. The connection to home remained the one fixed point in a life that was otherwise being remade, day by day, under military order.
